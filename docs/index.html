A pension pays retirement income to eligible former employees. A contribution is money paid into the pension plan to help fund those benefits. Employees may contribute too, and the plan invests its money.

Would FRS cost less?

Panama City operates local retirement programs alongside a grandfathered group in the state’s Florida Retirement System (FRS). At the reported employer contribution rates, FRS is higher for general employees and police and lower for fire. Net savings from a transition are not yet established.

This comparison applies both rates to the same hypothetical $1 million in annual covered payroll. It shows a contribution-rate difference, not the city’s projected annual savings or a comparison of equivalent benefits. City rates are from the November 9, 2026 memo; FRS rates took effect July 1, 2026 and can change.

Why a lower rate is not yet a savings estimate

Moving future retirement coverage to FRS would not simply erase benefits already earned under the city’s plans. A transition analysis must account for remaining local pension funding payments, any transfer or past-service costs, administration, and the timing of employee participation. The fire rate difference should not be treated as money available to spend elsewhere.

Employee costs and benefits also change. FRS generally requires a 3% employee contribution, compared with 0% for GERP, 8.13% for police and 7.5% for fire in the city memo. DROP participants contribute 0%. A lower employee contribution is not a city budget saving; benefit formulas, vesting and retirement eligibility also need comparison.

Two scenarios to evaluate

  • New hires join FRS: compare costs as the workforce changes while separately funding existing local benefits.
  • Existing employees transition: establish eligibility and employee choices, treatment of accrued benefits, and any transfer costs before estimating savings.

Both scenarios need verified covered payroll by FRS class, actuarial projections of remaining local obligations, transition costs and employee benefit comparisons. Neither scenario is assumed to be approved or immediately available.

The city’s 2024 general-employee study estimated roughly $730,000 more annually for FRS using about $20 million in payroll and then-current rates. That historical estimate is not a current forecast and does not establish police or fire savings.

Long-term pension funding

FY 2027 employer retirement contributions total $7.47 million citywide, including $6.52 million in the General Fund. A pension valuation estimates the value today of benefits employees have already earned and compares it with the plan’s assets: money and investments set aside to pay benefits.

Pension liability
The estimated value today of retirement benefits employees have already earned. Those benefits will be paid over many years. The estimate depends on assumptions about investment returns, pay and how long people live.
Actuarial valuation
A financial study by an actuary, a specialist who estimates future costs and risks. It helps determine how much the pension plan needs and what contributions should be paid.
Funded ratio
The plan’s assets divided by its estimated pension liability, using the study’s methods. A ratio of 76.1% means about $76.10 in assets for every $100 of estimated benefits earned. It does not mean retirees receive only 76.1% of their pensions.
Unfunded actuarial liability
The gap between the estimated liability and the assets counted in the funding study. It is a long-term funding shortfall, not a bill due all at once. Contributions and investment results affect how that gap changes.

What the city owes and pays each year

$229.69 million in outstanding principal at September 30, 2024, across city government and business-type activities. That audited figure covers bonds, notes and direct borrowings. The city’s broader outstanding debt schedule reports $234,437,765 for the same date, including financed purchases, leases and subscription liabilities. The adopted FY 2027 budget sets aside $15.26 million for debt service across all funds.

Debt outstanding is the balance still owed at a particular date. Annual debt service is the amount scheduled to be paid during one year. Think of a loan balance compared with a year of loan payments: they measure different things. The balance and budget figures here also come from different years.

What do the borrowing terms mean?
Principal and interest
Principal is the amount borrowed that must be repaid. Interest is the charge for using the lender’s money. Paying interest does not reduce principal.
Bonds, notes and direct borrowings
Ways for the city to borrow money and promise repayment. Bonds are sold to investors; notes and direct borrowings are other forms of loan agreements.
Financed purchases, leases and subscription liabilities
Amounts the city owes under certain pay-over-time purchases, agreements to use property or equipment, and software subscription contracts.
Refinancing or refunding debt
Replacing earlier borrowing with new borrowing. This can change payment dates or other terms; it does not by itself erase the debt or guarantee savings.
Maturity date
The date when the final principal payment is due under the borrowing agreement.

Actual expenses and adopted budgets are different measures. FY2027 amounts authorize spending; they do not show payments made or projects completed. These CRA funds are included in the city’s all-funds budget, so do not add them to that total again. Transfers may also appear in the receiving fund.

Budget words explained

Choose a topic for short definitions. The source links provide more detail. Tax and revenue definitions and expense and pension definitions appear beside their budget figures.

Budget plans, accounts and available money
Revenue and receipts
Revenue is money earned or collected from sources such as taxes and fees. Receipts are money actually received; the timing can differ from when revenue is recorded.
Fund and General Fund
A fund tracks money set aside for a purpose. The General Fund pays for everyday city services. Fund numbers, such as 150 or 190, identify accounts; they are not rankings.
Adopted, amended and actual
Adopted is the approved spending plan. Amended is that plan after approved changes. Actual is the amount recorded, rather than planned.
Fiscal year (FY) and year to date (YTD)
A fiscal year is an organization’s 12-month accounting period. Year to date runs from the start of that period through the report date.
Appropriation
Permission in the budget to spend up to a stated amount for a purpose. An unspent appropriation is unused spending authority, not proof that the same amount is available in the bank.
Reserves and fund balance
Reserves are resources held for future needs. Fund balance is an accounting measure of resources remaining after liabilities and certain timing adjustments. Neither term automatically means cash free to spend on anything.
Restricted, committed and available
Restricted money has limits imposed by law or an outside source. Committed money has been set aside by formal government action. Available money can be used for the stated purpose after applicable limits and obligations are considered.
Encumbered
Money reserved in the accounts for an order or contract that has not yet been fully paid. It may still be in the bank, but it is already spoken for.
Administration and nondepartmental expenses
Administration pays for managing a program, including staff and paperwork. Nondepartmental costs are recorded outside a single service department; the label alone does not tell you what was purchased.
Budgeted position and vacancy
A budgeted position is a job included in the spending plan. A vacancy is an unfilled job. A cost per budgeted position is not necessarily the cost per employee actually working or enrolled in insurance.

Property values and tax calculations
Assessed value
The property value used in the tax calculation before exemptions, after applicable assessment limits. It can differ from a likely selling price.
Taxable value and exemption
An exemption removes eligible value from taxation. Taxable value is what remains for that tax after exemptions. An exemption reduces the value taxed, not the bill dollar for dollar.
Homestead
A qualifying permanent home. Owners must meet eligibility and application rules to receive the homestead exemption.
Millage and mill
The property-tax rate. One mill means $1 of tax for every $1,000 of taxable value. For example, 5 mills on $100,000 of taxable value equals $500.
Levy and ad valorem
A levy is a tax imposed by a government. Ad valorem means based on value, as with property tax.
TRIM notice
The Notice of Proposed Property Taxes explains proposed taxes, values and hearing information. TRIM stands for Truth in Millage. It is not the final tax bill.
Baseline and estimate
A baseline is the starting point used for comparison. An estimate is a calculation based on stated assumptions, not a promise of a future bill.

Projects, grants and contracts
Infrastructure and capital improvement
Long-lasting public systems and facilities, such as roads, pipes and drainage. A capital improvement builds or substantially improves them.
Grant, award and allocation
A grant provides funding under conditions. An award approves funding or a contract; an allocation assigns an amount to a purpose. Neither alone proves money has been received or spent.
Reimbursement and local match
A reimbursement repays eligible costs already paid. A local match is the contribution the city must provide alongside a grant; allowable contributions depend on the agreement.
Approved award, change order and revised contract
The approved award is the original contract amount. A change order formally changes work, price or timing. The revised contract reflects approved changes.
Paid to date
Payments recorded through the report date. This can be less than the approved contract because work or payment is still pending.
Business-type activities
City services accounted for much like a business because customers pay fees, such as utilities. This is an accounting category, not a statement that the service is privately owned.

Housing programs and assistance
SHIP
State Housing Initiatives Partnership, Florida funding used locally for eligible home purchases, repairs and housing assistance.
HUD, CDBG and NSP
HUD is the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development. CDBG means Community Development Block Grant, which funds eligible community needs. NSP means Neighborhood Stabilization Program, created to help communities affected by foreclosures and abandoned homes.
Documentary stamp tax
A Florida tax on certain documents, including property deeds and some loan documents. Part of its revenue supports housing programs.
Owner-occupied rehabilitation
Repairs or improvements to a home lived in by its owner. It does not mean building an additional home.
Infill and demolition/reconstruction
Infill adds buildings on vacant or underused land within an existing community. Demolition/reconstruction removes a building and replaces it. A replacement home does not necessarily increase the number of homes.
Impact-fee assistance
Help paying fees intended to cover added demand on public systems from development, such as water and sewer capacity.
Second mortgage
An additional loan secured by the home, behind the first mortgage in payment priority. Housing assistance may use this arrangement; repayment or forgiveness depends on the program’s terms.
Low- and moderate-income
Program eligibility categories based on household income and size. The applicable program sets the limits; the labels do not mean one fixed income for everyone.
Housing Choice Voucher and HUD-VASH
A voucher helps an eligible household pay rent. HUD-VASH combines rental help for eligible homeless veterans with support services through Veterans Affairs.
Funding cycle and units assisted
A funding cycle identifies the period or batch of funding being reported. Units assisted counts housing receiving help under the report’s rules; it is not automatically a count of new homes completed that year.
Net homes added
New homes minus homes removed within the same area and period. Repairs to existing homes do not add to the count.

Reading spending records and financial reports
Ledger, account and allocation
A ledger lists accounting entries. An account groups entries by purpose or type. An allocation assigns some or all of a charge to an account. One purchase can produce several entries.
Vendor, invoice and receipt
A vendor sells goods or services. An invoice requests payment. A receipt documents a payment or purchase. A linked document still needs to be checked against the transaction.
Gross, net and credits
Gross is the amount before deductions. Net is after specified deductions. On the card pages, credits reduce spending. For example, a $100 charge and a $20 credit produce $80 net spending.
Journal adjustment and posting date
A journal adjustment changes the accounting records and may move a cost between accounts without a new purchase. The posting date is when an entry is recorded; it can differ from the purchase date.
Opening balance and reconciliation
An opening balance is the amount already in an account when a period starts. Reconciliation compares records and explains differences. An unexplained difference is a question to resolve, not proof of wrongdoing.
Balance sheet, assets and liabilities
A balance sheet shows what an organization has and owes at a date. Assets are resources it owns or controls. Liabilities are obligations it must settle.
Net income, net loss and cash flow
Income or loss compares revenue with expenses under the report’s accounting rules. Cash flow tracks money moving in and out. A reported loss is not necessarily the same as cash leaving the bank.
Depreciation and impairment
Depreciation spreads an asset’s cost over its useful life. Impairment records a significant, unexpected loss of an asset’s ability to provide service. These entries do not themselves mean a new purchase or payment occurred.
Audited, unaudited and provisional
Audited statements have been examined by an independent auditor. Unaudited figures have not completed that process. Provisional findings can change as evidence improves. An audit is not proof that every purchase was individually checked.
Not additive
The displayed amounts should not simply be added together. A purchase, the payment of its bill and transfers between accounts may describe overlapping parts of the same activity.
Likely and unresolved
On this site, Likely marks an inference supported by clues rather than confirmation. Unresolved means the evidence does not yet settle the question.

Abbreviations in the fund schedules
F.O.G.
Fats, oils and grease. The city’s program charges covered establishments and helps prevent grease from blocking sewer pipes.
Refuse and CNG
Refuse means garbage. CNG means compressed natural gas, a vehicle fuel.
Interfund and debt proceeds
Interfund means between city funds. Debt proceeds are money received from borrowing, which creates a repayment obligation.
CIP and refunding
CIP commonly means capital improvement program or plan. Refunding means replacing earlier debt with new borrowing. Years in debt-fund names identify financing issues, not necessarily the year of current spending.
Miscellaneous N.O.C.
A catch-all revenue label for items not otherwise classified. The label alone does not identify who paid or what each receipt was for.
Gross accounting basis
Totals include internal movements such as transfers between funds. The same outside dollar can appear in more than one fund, so the combined spending budget is not a count of unique dollars collected from the public.
