How per-resident and per-position costs are calculated

Per resident uses the 2025 Census estimate of 37,806 for all FY 2027 budget comparisons, not a FY 2027 population forecast. Per position uses the budget’s personnel schedule, which includes part-time and fractional positions. It is not actual headcount, employee pay or average retiree benefits. How much comes from pensions?

Contributions fund both benefits earned this year and obligations left from earlier years. They are already included in departmental spending—not an extra amount to add to it.

Why Fire pension contributions fund past obligations

Fire: most required funding goes toward past obligations

The October 2025 valuation calculates $4,264,560 in total required contributions for the year ending September 2027, before splitting the funding among the city, state and employees.

  • Paying down unfunded obligations · 68.8%$2,934,256
  • Benefits earned during the year · 29.7%$1,266,514
  • Administration · 1.5%$63,790

This actuarial requirement is a different measure from the city’s pension budget line. State and employee contributions, timing and budget assumptions need to be reconciled before calling any difference a shortfall.

See the local plan features

Fire plan

Normal retirement at age 55 with 10 years, or after 25 years of service at any age. The base formula is 2.9% of average final compensation per service year. It includes a temporary $575 monthly supplement and a cost-of-living provision that ends at Social Security retirement age.

Read the Fire plan’s conditions

Police plan

Normal retirement can begin at age 50 with 15 years, after 23 years at any age, or at age 55 with 10 years. Benefits follow a graduated schedule, reaching 73% of average final compensation at 25 years. Cost-of-living provisions vary with retirement date.

What is verified—and what is still missing?

Audited pension funds recognized city contributions of $3,673,936 in 2021 and $4,643,164 in 2024. These exclude state support, employee contributions and other retirement plans; they are not independently verified bank payments. State-reported Police and Fire spending rose from $21,575,755 to $35,767,044. The two series are not yet reconciled, so their increases cannot establish how much spending growth came from pensions. In 2024, budget contribution expenses differ from audited city-plus-state contributions by $5,710 for Police and $4,086 for Fire. The budget book also reports $541,547 less Police spending than the audit. These differences remain unresolved.
